A review that production already agrees with

A code review tells you what could happen. Production telemetry tells you what did. Almost nobody joins them — so findings arrive as opinion, and get prioritised by argument. Joining them changes which findings get fixed first, and how fast the room agrees.

00

The problem with reviews as they are normally run

Every engineer has received a review document and quietly ignored it. Not because the findings were wrong — because of how they arrived.

  • They read as opinion. A list of concerns with no way to tell which are real.
  • Severity is guesswork. Everything is High, so nothing is.
  • The team argues rather than fixes. Half the meeting is spent litigating whether an item counts.
  • Nobody knows what was not looked at. Silence gets read as a pass.
  • It goes stale immediately and is never re-run, because re-running means redoing it by hand.

The findings are perishable. The method is the asset.

Seven strategies fix those five failures. Each one is small. Together they change a review from a document people tolerate into a thing they act on.

01

Evidence discipline

The habit that makes a reviewer trusted

Every claim carries one of three markers. The third one is the whole idea.

MarkerMeansYou may
verifiedRead the code path end to end, in this workspaceRaise a finding
partialOne side is present; the counterpart lives elsewhereRaise a finding, stated as conditional
needs evidenceThe deciding code or config is not in the workspaceNot a finding. Record a gap naming the artifact required.
Why this earns the room

Needs-evidence is not "probably fine" and it is not "a vulnerability." It is a request with an address on it. Claiming a vulnerability you cannot prove loses the room permanently. Marking something clean that you could not actually see is worse — it is the one failure mode that discredits an entire review. Refusing to do either is what makes the findings you do raise land.

There is a second benefit nobody expects: the gap list becomes the most useful page in the document for a manager, because every item on it is something only they can unblock.

02

Dimensions, not vibes

"Review this repo" produces whatever the reviewer happened to notice. Nineteen named lenses — each with a checklist, its telltale patterns, and a defined scope — produce something comparable across repos and repeatable by someone else.

Security

secret-leak · token-integrity · token-storage · authz-decision · session-lifecycle · client-crypto · xss-surface · supply-chain · config-drift

Reliability

outbound-resilience · fail-mode · error-taxonomy · state-cleanup · delivery-semantics

Performance

data-access-perf · cache-correctness · concurrency · resource-leak · perf-render

Which dimensions run is decided by the repo's tier and kind, not by taste. A config repo and a browser app get genuinely different passes — and filing "no CSP" against a config repo costs you credibility for the findings that matter.

The detail that makes step 04 possible

Each dimension also declares what production signal it would produce — its symptom class. An outbound-resilience defect shows up as service-slowdown or timeout; data-access-perf shows up as a database hotspot. Writing that down once turns correlation from a judgement call into a join.

03

Findings that survive contact

Before anything is written down as a finding, it answers four questions. Most candidates die at question two or three, and that is the point.

  1. Where exactly? File, line, and the specific expression.
  2. What is the concrete failure? Real inputs or state, leading to a wrong outcome. If you cannot write that sentence, you have a code smell — not a finding.
  3. What guards it today? Walk up the call chain. A validation, a gateway policy, a framework default may already prevent it. If the guard lives outside what you can see, the finding drops to partial.
  4. Would a reasonable engineer disagree? If yes, write their objection into the finding — and answer it there.

A short list that holds up beats a long list that gets picked apart.

That fourth question is the one that changes meetings. When the owning team's best objection is already in the document with an answer, the conversation starts at "when do we fix it" instead of "is this even real".

04

The correlation model

The centrepiece — join the review to production

Every finding gets exactly one verdict against the last 30 days of telemetry. Five verdicts, because the obvious two are a trap.

VerdictCriteriaWhat it lets you say
observedSame service and a signature match — the problem names the exact statement, exception or endpoint"This is happening in production." No further persuasion needed.
consistentSame service, and a problem in this dimension's symptom class"Production shows the symptom this defect would produce." Suggestive, not proof.
no signalInstrumented, in scope, quiet for the window"Not observed in 30 days." Low frequency — never "not real".
blindUninstrumented, unmapped, or filtered out of the pull"We cannot see this either way." Absence proves nothing.
unobservableThis class of defect produces no telemetry even when exploited"Monitoring cannot corroborate this by design."
priority=severity×corroboration×blast radius
rank by this, not by severity alone

Telemetry promotes. It never absolves.

The trap this exists to prevent

A forged token returns 200. A leaked secret throws no exception. A missing authorization check succeeds every single time it is exploited. The most severe findings in most systems are exactly the ones a green dashboard is silent about — so silence is never allowed to demote them. Observed doubles a finding's priority; no-signal removes only a fifth; blind and unobservable remove nothing at all. That asymmetry is the ethic of the whole method, and it is the sentence to say out loud in the meeting.

05

Read it backwards

Correlation is bidirectional, and most people only run one direction. Walking the telemetry that matched no finding is where the genuinely new information is.

On a reviewed repo

Either the review missed something, or it is infrastructure rather than code. Say which — the first case is a gap to go close.

On an unreviewed repo

Production is telling you which repo to onboard next. That is a prioritisation signal you did not have to argue for.

On an unmapped service

Nobody has connected that service to a codebase. A gap in the estate map itself.

This is the section that stops the review being a one-way audit. It surfaces the reviewer's own misses, which is uncomfortable — and is exactly why the team believes the rest of it.

06

Coverage honesty

A board that shows only findings implies everything else is fine. Four things have to be as visible as the findings themselves: repos not yet reviewed, dimensions deliberately skipped, evidence gaps outstanding, and services where telemetry can see nothing at all.

Two words that must never be confused

Blind is not no-signal. One means "we looked and it was quiet". The other means "we cannot look". Conflating them turns a coverage report into a false assurance — and it is the single most common way this kind of exercise misleads the people who commissioned it.

There is a bonus in it. Collect the blind list across the estate and you have not written a caveat — you have written the observability roadmap, with each entry naming the service to instrument and why it matters. That is frequently the most actionable artifact the whole review produces, and it belongs to a team that never expected anything from you.

07

Repeatable, not heroic

Three weeks of one person reading code is not a process. It becomes one when the knowledge is split into three layers that change at different rates.

Method

Dimensions, verdicts, templates, the four questions. Generic. Never changes per client.

Profile

Architecture, vocabulary, tiers, sensitive paths, severity policy, out-of-scope. One file. Swappable.

Registry

Per repo: reviewed date, dimensions run, findings, evidence gaps, telemetry mapping. The only state.

Adding the twenty-second repo then stops being a favour someone does and becomes a defined pipeline: register it, map it to its telemetry (or declare it blind, deliberately), index it, queue its dimensions, review, correlate, update the index. A teammate can run it. So can you, six months from now, having forgotten everything.

If you are editing the method to fit a client, that content belongs in the profile instead.

08

How to present this

The arc — three acts, in this order
  1. The join, not the findings. Open with the idea: two evidence sources that are normally kept apart. You are not presenting a list of problems, you are presenting a way of knowing which problems are real. This framing decides whether the room listens or gets defensive in the first ninety seconds.
  2. One corroborated example, end to end. A single finding: here is the code, here is the failure, here is your own Dynatrace showing it. Watching a static finding turn into an observed incident is the moment the method sells itself. One example is enough — three is a lecture.
  3. The ask. Never end on findings. End on what you need: access to close specific gaps, an owner for the top items, agreement on what runs next.

09

A 20-minute walkthrough

If you get a team slot, this is the shape. It survives being cut to ten minutes by dropping items 4 and 6.

0–2
The idea

Two evidence sources normally kept apart. Say the thesis, name the scope, do not show a single finding yet.

2–5
One finding, end to end

Code → failure scenario → the telemetry that names it. The whole pitch lives or dies here, so pick the cleanest example you have.

5–9
The five verdicts and the asymmetry

Why silence never absolves. Use the forged-token line — it is the one people repeat afterwards.

9–12
The priority queue

Top five only. Named owner suggestion against each. Resist showing the full list.

12–15
Coverage and blind spots

What was reviewed, what was skipped, what nothing can see. Hand the blind list to the platform team in the room.

15–17
How it repeats

Method / profile / registry. Adding repo twenty-two is a pipeline, not a favour. Thirty seconds, no tooling demo.

17–20
The ask

Access for the named gaps. Owners for the top five. A decision on the remaining repos. End here, not on findings.

The one line to rehearse

If you say nothing else and they remember one sentence, make it this: "Production telemetry is allowed to raise the alarm on a finding. It is never allowed to grant absolution." It is short, it is memorable, and it is the whole method compressed.

Method briefing. Deliberately contains no findings, file paths or service names — the method travels, the findings do not.
